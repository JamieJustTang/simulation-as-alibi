# Case Studies and Stakeholder Analysis: Findings III

To examine how the simulation as alibi mechanism operates across institutional domains, we selected three cases using a systematic variation design: the object of analysis (written social order plus emergence framing) is held constant while the institutional domain---commercial, military, governmental---varies. Each case thus functions as an independent test of whether the alibi pattern is domain-specific or structurally general. We selected Artificial Societies as our commercial case not because it is the most representative platform---there is no representative platform in this nascent domain---but because it offers the highest documentation transparency, making the procedural architecture more discoverable than in comparable platforms. We selected the EU Emergency Management case as a *prospective* rather than retrospective case: it illustrates the preventive value of UDOS by showing what cannot be disclosed under current practice before deployment, rather than documenting harms after they have occurred.

**Case A: Commercial --- Artificial Societies.** Artificial Societies (founded 2024) provides population-scale social simulation as a platform service to municipal governments, academic institutions, and corporate clients. The platform's published documentation describes agents that "develop emergent cooperation and resource-sharing norms through repeated local interaction". Our analysis of the technical documentation reveals a highly authored architecture: (1) interaction topology is a directed graph defined by the platform, with asymmetric communication capacities---some agent types can broadcast, others can only receive; (2) the action grammar excludes several classes of behavior that exist in real human societies, including collective bargaining, regulatory appeal, and platform exit; (3) the sanction architecture for antisocial behavior (e.g., hoarding, exclusion) is partially automated and partially governed by platform-defined thresholds; (4) the beneficiary of the simulation is contractually defined: the paying client (typically a government agency) receives the outputs, while the simulated citizens have no representation in the design process. This is the epistemic laundering and accountability displacement mechanisms operating in tandem: the platform's language of emergence provides the epistemic cover, while the authored architecture remains commercially protected.

**Case B: Military --- Department of War/Ender's Foundry.** The U.S. Department of War (the pre-1947 designation, adopted here to reflect the historical continuity of military AI governance) has integrated LLM agent-based simulation into battlefield management and strategic planning since 2024, most notably through the Ender's Foundry program. Publicly available documentation describes a multi-agent system in which simulated adversarial and allied units "develop emergent tactical coordination patterns." The governance concern here is not merely that harm could occur but that the architecture that conditions tactical recommendations is classified---the action grammar, sanction architecture, and interaction topology are military secrets. This case represents the extreme form of the alibi pattern: even a well-intentioned auditor with full security clearance would face the challenge of distinguishing emergent tactical novelty from architecturally conditioned outputs, because the architecture is structurally unavailable to analysis. Accountability displacement here operates not through discourse alone but through the institutional infrastructure of classification that makes the authored conditions of simulation output doubly invisible---first by the language of emergence, second by national security law. We emphasize the inferential chain supporting this claim: the inference to classification is drawn from the systematic absence of architectural description in public documentation, triangulated with established military procurement practices in which interaction topologies and action grammars are routinely treated as operational security assets. The argument proceeds from observable documentation patterns, through institutional context, to the structural condition of invisibility---rather than from omission directly to intentional concealment.

**Case C: Governmental --- EU Emergency Management.** The European Union has deployed LLM agents for cross-border emergency response coordination, including resource allocation, evacuation planning, and inter-agency communication prioritization during simulated crisis scenarios. As a prospective case, the central finding is not that harm has occurred but that the current framework provides no mechanism for the public or the European Parliament to know *who wrote the rules* for resource allocation in a crisis. If a simulation prioritizes resource distribution to certain regions or populations, is this because the agents independently developed this allocation pattern (emergence) or because the interaction topology embedded a specific distributional logic (architecture)? The question is structurally unanswerable under current governance. This is the governance outsourcing mechanism: the procedural architecture that encodes allocative decisions functions as a de facto policy instrument, yet its authorship is insulated from democratic oversight by the same emergence narrative that operates in commercial and military domains.

Table (outsourcing modes) summarizes the pattern of outsourcing across the three cases. Epistemic outsourcing appropriates academic terminology to present proprietary, classified, or undocumented architectures as scientifically validated discoveries. Accountability outsourcing attributes consequential outcomes to emergent agent dynamics rather than to authored design choices. Governance outsourcing insulates architecture-level design from oversight through platform self-regulation, national security exemption, or technical delegation.

 
A cross-case stakeholder analysis yields a convergent conclusion. In each domain, the distribution of power with respect to the procedural authorship gap is the same: the actors who could close the gap---platform executives, defense contractors, government agencies---derive benefit from its persistence. Artificial Societies' commercial value depends on the proprietary nature of its interaction architecture; the DoD's tactical advantage depends on the classification of its agent network; the EU Commission's operational flexibility depends on the absence of parliamentary architecture-level oversight. The single sentence that summarizes the stakeholder finding across all three cases is: *those who have the power to close the procedural authorship gap benefit from its continuation.* This distributional stability is not a conspiracy but a structural equilibrium: the alibi pattern persists not because any single actor intentionally maintains it but because the costs of maintaining designer invisibility are borne by those without power to change the terms of the simulation.

We pause here to make explicit the normative premise that bridges our findings to the governance proposal in Section 7. If procedural authorship is invisible, the accountability chain is broken at its source. If those who benefit from this invisibility hold the power to maintain it, the gap will not close through self-correction. Therefore, intervention at the *upstream* design stage---where procedural architecture is authored before simulation runs---is the most effective leverage point for restoring the accountability chain, because it is the only point at which design choices are still articulable as choices rather than as emergent properties of a running system. Downstream intervention (deployment monitoring, output auditing, post-hoc harm assessment) is structurally incapable of recovering the design conditions that have already been rendered invisible through the emergence narrative. The most effective place to intervene is where the rules are written, not where their consequences are observed. We turn now to UDOS, which operationalizes this insight into a concrete governance framework.
